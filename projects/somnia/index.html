Somnia

Joel Brown | May 3, 2025 min read

Every human culture that has ever existed has had a word for dreams.

The ancient Egyptians built temples where the sick would sleep on sacred ground, waiting for a vision that would tell them how to heal. The Greeks called it incubation — sleeping inside the oracle. Aboriginal Australians don’t separate the Dreaming from reality at all. Jung spent a lifetime mapping the unconscious and concluded that what appears in sleep is the self attempting to speak to itself in the only language honest enough: symbol, feeling, image.

We all do this. Every night, without exception. And most mornings, we forget before we’ve even stood up.

Somnia is a dream journal that listens back.

Not in the way that sounds like a pitch. In the way that a therapist notices something across twelve sessions that you couldn’t see in any one of them. The recurring shape of your anxiety. The person who keeps appearing. The feeling of falling right before something in your waking life changes.

You write the dream. Somnia holds it. Over time, the AI begins to surface what your conscious mind has been too busy to notice — recurring themes, emotional undercurrents, symbols that cluster around particular periods of your life. Not as diagnosis. As reflection.

You already know. This helps you hear it.

The Practice

Log a dream the moment you wake — before the details dissolve. Rate how it felt. Mark whether you were aware inside it. Add the tags that feel right.

When you’re ready, ask for an analysis. The AI was designed to offer observations, not conclusions. It notices. It asks. It offers interpretations as possibilities, not certainties — because the meaning of a dream belongs to the dreamer. It ends with a quote drawn from whatever tradition resonates with you: Jungian psychology, Eastern philosophy, sacred texts, practical wisdom. Whatever lens you use to make sense of being alive.

Over weeks and months, the Insights page becomes something else entirely. A map of your inner weather. Dominant emotions across a hundred nights. Symbols that recur. The arc of your mood rendered as a quiet line chart. The things your subconscious keeps returning to.

Your Dreams Stay Yours

Dreams are perhaps the most private thing a person produces. They contain fears you haven’t admitted, grief you haven’t processed, desires you haven’t named. The idea of that data sitting unprotected on a server somewhere is worth taking seriously.

Every dream in Somnia is encrypted on your device before it leaves it — AES-256, the same standard used by Signal and 1Password. What reaches the database is ciphertext. Even during AI analysis, your dream is decrypted only for the moment it takes to interpret, then gone. No advertising. No data brokering. No analytics SDKs watching how long you spend on each screen.

Your encryption key is backed up locally so a server reset can never take your history from you.

What you’re owed when you trust an app with your subconscious: that it treats that trust seriously. Somnia was built with that as the constraint, not an afterthought.

What is stored: your encrypted dream content, mood and lucidity ratings, AI analysis results (encrypted), and your subscription status.

What is never stored: unencrypted dream text, device identifiers, location, or anything that doesn’t need to exist.

Where It Lives

Somnia is live on the Google Play Store, currently in closed beta. The foundation is Flutter with a Supabase backend — chosen because the data should be yours, not locked into a platform that could disappear. The AI runs on edge functions that process and discard. The freemium model exists because the infrastructure has real costs, not to extract value from people exploring something as human as why they dream.

The goal, eventually, is both Android and iOS. The deeper goal is to build something people return to not because it sends them notifications, but because the practice is worth keeping.

Sleep is the one thing we all do. Dreams are the part of it that speaks. Somnia is for people who want to learn what theirs are saying.
